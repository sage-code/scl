    Incident Response

    Incidents are inevitable; chaotic incidents are not. A practiced response process turns a stressful outage into a sequence of familiar steps: detect, assess, coordinate, mitigate, communicate, and learn. This lesson covers on-call readiness, triage and severity, incident roles, communication patterns, and the blameless postmortem that keeps the same incident from happening twice.

    On-Call Readiness

    The quality of an incident response is mostly decided before the incident starts: by who is on call, what they know, and what tools and documentation they have.

    Sustainable Rotations

    • Rotation size: enough people that each is on call at most one week in four or five; fewer causes burnout.
    • Primary and secondary: a second person to escalate to if the primary does not acknowledge in a set time, typically 5–15 minutes.
    • Follow the sun: teams in several time zones can hand over so nobody is paged at night routinely.
    • Handover: a short note at each rotation change: open issues, risky changes planned, alerts that misbehaved.
    • Compensation and load: recognize on-call work, and track pages per shift; if nights are regularly broken, fixing alerts is the top priority.

    Be Ready Before the Page

    Before someone's first shift, they should have working access to every system they might need (tested, not assumed), know where the dashboards, runbooks and service ownership list are, have shadowed an experienced engineer, and have joined at least one practice incident. Every alert should link to a runbook that says what the alert means, how to confirm it, and the first safe actions.

    Detection and Triage

    An incident is any unplanned event that degrades a service, or threatens to. It may be detected by an alert, by a customer, or by an engineer who notices something odd. The first minutes are for triage, not for deep debugging.

    Assign a Severity

    SeverityDefinitionResponse
    SEV1Critical service down or data loss/exposure for many users.All hands; incident commander; status updates every 15–30 minutes; leadership informed.
    SEV2Major feature degraded, or a subset of users significantly affected.Incident commander and responders; regular updates.
    SEV3Minor impact, workaround available.Handled by on-call during working hours.

    Write the definitions down with concrete examples, so severity is decided in seconds rather than debated. When in doubt, declare the higher severity; downgrading later is cheap, while under-reacting to a growing incident is expensive.

    The First Ten Minutes

    1. Acknowledge the page so others know someone is on it.
    2. Assess impact: who is affected, how badly, since when? Check the service overview dashboard and SLO status.
    3. Declare an incident with a severity, open a dedicated channel, and start a timeline.
    4. Ask what changed: recent deployments, configuration changes, feature flags, dependency incidents.
    5. Call for help early if the problem is outside your area or the impact is large.

    Incident Roles

    Large incidents fail when everyone debugs and nobody coordinates. Borrowing from emergency services' incident command system, assign explicit roles; in small incidents one person may hold several.

    Who Does What

    RoleResponsibility
    Incident commanderOwns the incident: sets priorities, assigns tasks, decides on mitigations, and keeps the response moving. Does not debug.
    Operations lead / respondersInvestigate and make changes to systems, reporting to the commander before risky actions.
    Communications leadWrites internal and external updates on a schedule; shields responders from questions.
    ScribeKeeps the timeline: observations, decisions, actions and their times.

    The commander role transfers explicitly (“I am handing command to Maria”), never by drift. Long incidents need shift changes too: a tired responder makes mistakes.

    Mitigate First, Diagnose Later

    The goal during an incident is to restore service, not to find the root cause. Prefer fast, reversible mitigations: roll back the last deployment, turn off a feature flag, fail over to a replica, scale up, shed non-essential load, or block an abusive client. Many incidents are resolved before anyone fully understands them; the investigation continues calmly afterwards, with the timeline and telemetry preserved.

    Communication Patterns

    Poor communication makes an incident feel worse than it is and creates a second problem: people interrupting responders to ask what is happening.

    Internal Updates

    Use one incident channel per incident and keep side conversations out of it. Post updates on a fixed rhythm, even when there is no news, in a consistent format:

    **[SEV2] Checkout errors — update 3 (10:15 UTC)**
**Impact:** ~8% of checkout attempts fail with an error since 09:41 UTC. Browsing is unaffected.
**Current status:** Errors began with release 2.4.1. Rollback to 2.4.0 is in progress.
**Next steps:** Confirm error rate returns to baseline after rollback.
**Next update:** 10:45 UTC or sooner if status changes.
**Commander:** @maria  · **Channel:** #inc-2026-11-14-checkout

    Customer-Facing Status

    Update the public status page early — customers are more forgiving of a problem than of silence. Describe impact in user terms, avoid speculation about causes, never blame a vendor publicly during the incident, and give the time of the next update. When resolved, say so, and later publish a summary of what happened and what will change, if the impact justifies it.

    Postmortems

    An incident not followed by learning is likely to recur. The postmortem (also called a post-incident review) turns a painful event into lasting improvement.

    Blameless by Design

    A blameless postmortem assumes that people acted reasonably given what they knew at the time, and asks how the system — tools, processes, defaults, documentation — made the failure possible and recovery slow. “An engineer ran the wrong command” leads to the better questions: why did the command allow that, why was it run by hand, why did nothing catch it? Blame makes people hide information, and hidden information is how incidents repeat.

    Write the Review

    # Postmortem: Checkout errors after release 2.4.1 (2026-11-14)

## Summary
For 41 minutes, ~8% of checkout attempts failed. Release 2.4.1 reduced the payment
client's timeout from 10 s to 1 s; the provider's p99 latency is 1.6 s.

## Impact
3,120 failed checkouts; 19% of the monthly error budget consumed. No data loss.

## Timeline (UTC)
- 09:38 Release 2.4.1 deployed to 100% (no canary stage for this service)
- 09:41 Error rate rises; 09:47 burn-rate alert pages on-call
- 10:02 Incident declared SEV2; release identified as suspect
- 10:19 Rollback complete; 10:22 error rate back to baseline

## Contributing factors
- Timeout value changed without a load test against real provider latency.
- Service deploys straight to 100%; a 5% canary would have limited impact.

## Action items
| Action | Owner | Due |
|---|---|---|
| Add canary stage to checkout pipeline | @lee | 2026-11-28 |
| Alert on payment provider p99 latency | @maria | 2026-11-21 |
| Document timeout budget per dependency | @sam | 2026-12-05 |

    Hold the review within a few days while memories are fresh, invite everyone involved, and track action items in the normal work backlog with owners and dates. Review completion rates: postmortems whose actions never ship are theater.

    Practicing Response

    Teams that rehearse respond faster and more calmly. Practice is cheap compared with learning during a real SEV1.

    Game Days and Tabletop Exercises

    A tabletop exercise walks a team through a scenario in conversation: “the primary database is unreachable — what do you do first?” A game day injects a real failure into a test environment, or carefully into production, and runs the full process: alert, declaration, roles, communication and postmortem. Both reveal missing access, stale runbooks and unclear ownership while the stakes are low.

    Practice Lab

    Work in a group of three or four; one person acts as the facilitator who knows the scenario.

    Run a Tabletop Incident

    1. The facilitator prepares a scenario with a timeline of symptoms, such as rising latency followed by errors after a configuration change.
    2. Assign commander, responder, communications and scribe roles, then work the incident aloud for thirty minutes as the facilitator reveals clues.
    3. Post at least three status updates in the template above, and decide on and justify one mitigation.

    Write the Postmortem

    1. Using the scribe's timeline, write a blameless postmortem with impact, contributing factors and three action items.
    2. For each action item, state which future incident it prevents or shortens.
    3. Rewrite one sentence that blames a person so it describes the system condition instead.
